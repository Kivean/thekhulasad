## Haid and Agïs
Any sufficiently complex system can store magical energy. In essence, the transfer of information between units wears paths into the fabric of the world. These paths resonate with the magic energies that permeate the world, storing energy which can be directed and shaped.  Once thought to be purely the domain of living beings, and called "*Nesu*", the force of life, later societies discovered that any system could generate magical energy. Now commonly called "*haid*", meaning "Flow", it describes not only the energy itself, but also the paths of information ("*agïs*", literally "channels worn in rock by flowing water") flow between units.  

As a point of historical interest, the word "*haid*" derives from several words for menstruation, as the menses were likened to the accumulation of magical energy by early practioners, who would go many days without performing magic, and then be exhausted afterwards.

To harness Flow to perform magic, there is always a cost.  Flow must be spent.  The resonances of a system may only store certain amounts of "surplus" energy, afterwards, spellcasting harms the *agïs* directly, weakening the system, and possibly harming the ability of the system to remain coherent, or generate Flow.  For example, a common spell taught to outriders of the first badlands cities was one to turn organic life, usually plants or small animals, into stone.  The spell would destroy the chosen target, consuming its Flow and releasing the passive energy stored in the *agïs*. Powered also by the Flow of the caster, a rock matrix could be grown to fill gaps or holes. Such transmutation was possible for many pairs of target and product, although "flow inert" things, such as most rocks and metals, carry low latent Flow.  To transform, for example, lead to gold, would require a significant expenditure of Flow on the part of the caster.  In contrast, harnessing even the comparatively modest Flow present in a plant would make it easier to generate either lead or gold.  

Additional factors, and more nebulous *agïs* connections, can have significant effects on the utility of magics. As an example, a pile of weeds, torn from their contexts, have less available Flow than they would if they were in the place they grew.  

## Aïnhaz
All of this ties into the related concept of *aïnhaz*, a sort of magical ecology.  Aïnhaz means, roughly, "alignment" and describes the "type" of magical milieu a place possesses.  Typically used to refer to land, it indicates what kind of place one can expect.  A cave inhabited by giant spiders for many thousands of years might possess an *aïnhaz* which is extremely hostile to, say, your average adventurer.  This typically manifests in "feelings" about the nature of a place (welcoming, foreboding, disinterested, etc.) and skilled druids and rangers, for example, can typically tell a lot about a place by its *aïnhaz*.  

It is common knowledge that *aïnhaz* is only generated by lands and natural systems which do not experience consciousness.  There are fringe scholars, ideologues, and practitioners who hold that there is no firm dividing line between those things which are conscious and those which are not.  Some go so far as to suggest that cities, or even individual homes may have their own *aïnhaz*, which is a most frightening heresy and a danger to the humanoid races.  For a simple proof against this concept, take a tree in the wild, a tree in an orchard, and a tree in a city.  The wild tree grows ragged and inhospitable to us.  The orchard tree, grown to harvest the *aïnhaz* of the land under it, grows largely regularly, and gives fruit.  The city tree grows broad and shady, larger than the orchard tree and much more so than the wild tree, with even limbs and straight trunk; a testament to the fact that the humanoid races alone have the ability to expunge the *aïnhaz* of a region and bring about order.
