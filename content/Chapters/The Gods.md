To the less enlightened cultures of the world, there are only gods and peons.  In truth, most "deific" magic is actually performed with the help of extremely powerful entities (enormous magical creatures, powerful computers, the Sisters, etc.) rather than true deities.  The line between the two is simultaneously clear and indistinct.  Deities have, apparently, no absolute power over anything, but simply possess a quantity of *[[Stories/Pre-Joman Tabletop Setting/Chapters/Magic#Haid and Agïs|haid]]* which is in vast excess of even the most powerful "terrestrial" entities.  For the purposes of nearly any reader of this text, the distinction between true deities and "deific entities" lies not in the power they hold, but in their willingness or even desire to intervene in affairs on the scale of the humanoids and their cities.

Perhaps better considered to be "entities" or even "systems" than "people", the gods are rarely seen in the world, and their direct impacts are also comparatively rare.  Whether the indirect impacts of the gods are either so prevalent as to be ubiquitous or so rare as to be obscure remains a source of great debate.  This debate is typically highly dividing between the *Jaks* and *Katibeh* (who hold the first and second views, respectively).  The [[The People#The Sahida|Sahida]] record that, nearly 3000 years ago, the movements which would become the Jaks and Katibeh split from a single scholastic faction over this very debate. When deities do present themselves, it is generally in highly abstracted forms which, paradoxically, typically engage with the peoples of the world directly.  What is consistent, however, is that deific impacts do appear, it is nearly always through the application of *[[Stories/Pre-Joman Tabletop Setting/Chapters/Magic#Haid and Agïs|haid]]* rather than a direct physical interaction.

Most scholars agree that no deity is any more "in control" of the physical phenomena of the world than any human, although their agency in controlling their surroundings is, of course, extremely different.  Put another way, while most gods are associated with a particular phenomenon or concept, they are not the supreme arbiter of that domain.

For the purposes of this chapter, two categories of entities will be put forth for consideration: the "true" deities, Gods, [[The Gods#Ars'Asmusstakdam - Gods|Ars'Asmusstakdam]], and the higher powers, Demigods, [[The Gods#Ars'Mandu - Demigods|Ars'Mandu]].  An important distinction made here is the elimination of powers based solely on *[[Stories/Pre-Joman Tabletop Setting/Chapters/Magic#Aïnhaz|aïnhaz]]*.  A powerful storm-caster may draw *agïs* from a strong storm, or may sing the *aïnhaz* of the storm into striking their target, but the storm is not a deity.
## Worship and Patronage
The gods are, to varying degrees, present or absent from the lives of people, but the peoples of Thaja, by their nature, are a constant fixture in the existence of the gods.  Whether asking boons, seeking empowerment, or simply maintaining a relationship, there are as many forms of worship as there are forms of the gods themselves.  Spiritual traditions, therefore, as varied by deity as they are by culture.  For discussions of common spiritual or religious practices in any given culture, see [[The People]].  Below are discussions of the traditions and practices common to the deities in questions.  

There are, broadly, two main ways in which the peoples of Thaja interact with the gods: worship and patronage.  The two are distinguished by the degree of directness in communication and the way in which empowerments and boons are commonly granted.  While there is significant overlap, *worship* is common among nearly all the peoples of Thaja and is typically characterized by little interaction from the deity, while *patronage* is rarer and typically involves, at the least, communication with the deity.
### Worship
Worship is performed as an act of connection with a deity through many forms, all typically with the goal of forging deeper *[[Stories/Pre-Joman Tabletop Setting/Chapters/Magic#Haid and Agïs|agïs]]* between the person (or community) and the deity.  As nearly all manifestations of deific will emerge through the effects of *haid*, it is understood that those with the deepest *agïs* are able to best benefit from the efforts of the deity.  Likewise, it is known that some deities (e.g. [[The Gods#Irhueb - God of Terror|Irhueb]]) value a deep *agïs* as a way to spread their influence more effectively through subtle manipulations of their followers or clergy.

Worship can typically be formed into two categories: clerical and non-clerical.  Clerical worship is an organized practice wherein members form structured groups and foster their *agïs* in prescribed ways.  At its most rigid, as in the temple structure of [[The Gods#Prunuee|Prunuee]], this means that followers attain certain ranks through attaining explicitly descried milestones of experience and ritual.  Clergy of higher rank are beholden to educate and motivate their followers, establish temples, and formally administer certain rites.  Most clerical institutions rely on permanent structures (e.g. temples) or other physical spaces (e.g. groves) as gathering places, although some (e.g. [[The Gods#Rappala - God of Intangibles|Rappala]]) mandate that clerical actions happen outside the material plane.

The scholars of the *Silattvik* consider the term "[[The Classes#Overview|cleric]]" to only refer to those in organized temple clergy, but the hallmark of the cleric is the ability to use the wisdom of their spiritual bond to call upon their god's *agïs.* Clerics, then, who follow those gods who do not follow clerical traditions, of which there are obviously many, are what?  They are, clearly, also clerics.

>For once, Tarjali pedantry reveals a genuine insight...

It is a common misconception that a cleric's personal *agïs* is the reservoir for their spellcasting.  Instead, it is the depth of the *agïs* between them and their deity which allows for a trickle of the deity's enormous *haid* reservoir to be conveyed to a target.  [[The Classes#Overview|Warpriests]], [[The Classes#Overview|inquisitors]], and some [[The Classes#Overview|paladins]] all cast spells this way.  This explains why some of the greatest clerics of legend were poor arcane spellcasters, and likewise why many of the greatest [[The Classes#Overview|wizards]] have never mastered divine magic.  This distinction is well understood by clerics and wizards themselves, as illustrated by the following descriptions:

*"One never feels the power of spellcasting more than in true resurrection.  When you let your spirit quiet, allow your heart to touch a fallen soul in the immaterium, there is an immediate, exultant urge to hold on, to grip the very stuff of a beloved friend and never let go. Instead, all one needs to do is gently cradle it, smile, and humbly ask for it to stay with you for just a moment.  Then it comes, a coil of the firmament of the gods themselves, a power which surrounds you and dissembles the fabric of all that lies just beyond your skin.  It smiles to you, you smile to it.  All you need to do is open your eyes to find it done, a soul returned to you, no more effort than it takes to fall asleep."*
Pthera Dun, 19,203

Dun was a cleric of [[The Gods#Fend - God of Growth|Fend]] who is notable for her sizeable following as a teacher and her significant accomplishments during the *Frest lal Kahbir.*



### Patronage
Patronage, in contrast to worship, is a *state* of connection with a deity, often achieved through worship, bargaining, pacts, or simple convenience.  Patronage is much rarer than worship, and some deities for whom worship is common almost never grant patronage (e.g. [[The Gods#Part|Part]]), or *vice versa* (e.g. [[The Gods#Balk|Balk]]).  It is also commonly assumed, due to the rarity of patronage, that it somehow conveys a status in relation to clergy.  This is generally untrue, and in fact, the reverse is often true.  In traditions with rigid clerical practices, those that have received patronage are typically considered outsiders.

## Ars'Mandu - Demigods
### Part
Extremely rare as a patron, common to worship.  Creator and destroyer, bringer of life, burner of skin.  Sun cults are ubiquitous across Thaja with diverse practices and traditions.  Much debate over whether clerics of Part are actually empowered by Part, empowered by Part against its will by the interference of Thaja, or just empowered by Thaja harnessing Part's energy.  Extremely rare as a warlock patron.  While several are recorded and confirmed, it is commonly believed that Part's view of Thajan life is simply too conflicted to warrant patronage. Clerical: yes.

### The Sisters
The Sisters all have their own personalities.
#### Thaja
NG
Rare as a patron, ubiquitous as a deity.  Thaja is the lifegiver, but also the ultimate impartial judge.  She contains all which is the foundation of life, but also the forces that will bring the end of most of us.  It is the rare Tarjali house that does *not* have some way to revere Thaja.  Thaja is inextricably intertwined in the lives of all who live upon her.  As a result, she attempts, to some degree, to maintain some distance, to not play favorites.  No one upon her considers praying for rain, and there is truly some question of whether she truly controls any of that anyway.  It's generally assumed that she fears the fate of *Vilfye* befalling her, but debate remains if that threat comes from beyond or within her skies. Ask a Thajan warlock or bosker, or especially a witch, and they'll tell you Thaja fears nothing.  Ask an oracle and they'll tell you fear is not the right word to use.  Clerical: no, too common  
#### Balk
NG
Common as a patron, rare as a deity.  The "chattiest" of the sisters, perhaps because her people still talk with those on Thaja.  Balk bizarrely, has a reputation for being cold and calculating, hence her rarity as a deity.  Talk to anyone who has her as a patron, or those few Balkeh sects, and they'll tell you a different story.  Known for appearing in quiet, meditative dreams, she is mostly concerned with the preservation of races she has first hand experience with and the cultivation of cults which venerate the sisters. Firmly contrary to her terrestrial reputation, Balk is well known among warlocks as a "safe patron"--meet a Balk follower and they'll probably not be interested in killing you. Clerical: not formally, although she promotes gatherings 
#### Gzethya
LN
Rare as a patron, extremely rare as a deity.  The "forgotten sister", she shared an extremely precise 2:5 orbital resonance with Vilfye (hence the similar etymological origins of their names) and was, apparently, the "hardest hit" by Vilfye's destruction.  Her dark surface and her position off the ecliptic (slowly getting better after Vilfye) make her hard to see and so she's not often thought of.  Warlocks of Gzethya are typically paranoid, as Gzethya is endlessly paranoid some Thajan or Balkeh plot will see her or another of her sisters obliterated. Clerical: no, too rare
#### Prunuee
LG
Rare as a patron, common as a deity.  Despite being the farthest sister, Prunuee is the largest and the brightest by far, making her a consistent nighttime sight and a common object of worship. She is an aloof, principled sister, but she takes seriously her role as a protector of those smaller than her. It is commonly held that she is uncommonly massive for her size and this grants her enormous power.  Her rarity as a patron reflects nothing but her exacting standards.  It is also commonly held that she and Part have a begrudging respect for each other, although her warlocks and clergy may say otherwise. Clerical: yes, with a rigid structure

CN
